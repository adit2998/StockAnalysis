# Reading a 10-K Like an Analyst
### How to *infer*, not just *read*

The amateur reads one 10-K and ticks boxes. The analyst reads **three to five years of 10-Ks side by side**, treats the document as something written by lawyers and executives who are *managing your impression*, and constantly asks two questions:

1. **What is this telling me that they didn't mean to tell me?**
2. **Does the story stay consistent across every section — and against the actual numbers?**

Most of the value is in (a) the **footnotes**, (b) the **MD&A**, and (c) the **year-over-year *changes* in language**. The headline statements are the part everyone already knows. Below, each section is framed as *the inference* — what a given disclosure actually signals.

---

## Item 1 — Business: read it as a confession of strategy

This section looks like marketing. Read it forensically.

- **Year-over-year language drift is the highest-signal thing in the whole item.** Pull last year's 10-K and this year's side by side. Companies quietly rewrite the business description. If a product line gets dropped from the narrative, it's being deprioritized or wound down. If the language around a segment becomes more hedged or vaguer, that segment is struggling and they don't want to say so directly. New emphasis = where they're pushing capital.
- **Customer concentration.** "No single customer exceeds 10% of revenue" is comfortable. A named customer at 25–40% is a *hostage situation* — that customer dictates pricing and can end the company by leaving. **Track the trend**: is concentration rising or falling each year?
- **The order they list segments and products** tells you what they want your attention on. They lead with the story they're selling.
- **How they define their market / TAM** reveals how they think about runway. A company that defines its market narrowly is signaling maturity; one that suddenly redefines it expansively may be papering over slowing core growth.
- **Supplier and input concentration** — single-source suppliers, key components, reliance on one foundry/manufacturer. This is supply-chain fragility hiding in plain sight.
- **Backlog / bookings (if disclosed)** — forward demand visibility. *Compare backlog growth to revenue growth*: backlog shrinking while revenue holds up means future revenue is rolling over.
- **Human capital disclosures** (now required) — headcount trends, attrition/turnover, and the *mix* (are they cutting R&D heads while growing sales? That's harvesting, not investing). A jump in attrition is a culture and competitiveness tell.
- **Intellectual property** — patent expiry dates (the pharma "patent cliff"), dependence on licensed-in technology you don't own.
- **Geographic mix** — exposure to FX, single-country regulatory or political risk.
- **Seasonality** — so you don't misread a structurally weak quarter as a problem.

> **The inference move:** Item 1 is the bull case in management's own words. Your job is to find where the bull case is thinning by comparing it to last year's version.

## Item 1A — Risk Factors: the delta is the data

Eighty percent of this is boilerplate written by lawyers to prevent lawsuits. The skill is filtering for the 20% that's real.

- **Read this year against last year and isolate what *changed*.** Newly added risk factors are the prize — a company only adds a specific new risk when it has become probable enough that omitting it creates legal exposure. That's the lawyers telling you what management is genuinely worried about *now*. Removed risks mean something resolved or stopped mattering. **Reordering** matters too — many companies put the scariest risk first.
- **Distinguish specific from generic.** Generic: "economic conditions may affect demand." Specific (and worth reading): risks that name a competitor, a dependency, a dollar figure, a facility, a regulator, or a pending investigation. Specificity = a real exposure.
- **Risks sometimes leak facts found nowhere else** — reliance on a single manufacturing site, a key patent's expiration date, an ongoing regulatory inquiry, customer concentration management downplayed in Item 1.

> **The inference move:** treat the risk-factor *diff* between years as a near-direct readout of management's evolving private worry list.

## Item 3 + the Contingencies footnote: cross-reference them

Read **Legal Proceedings (Item 3)** together with the **Commitments & Contingencies footnote** in the financials. The footnote quantifies what Item 3 describes — look for "reasonably possible" loss language, disclosed ranges of potential loss, and whether reserves have been taken. A *pattern* of recurring litigation (product liability, labor, IP) is a structural cost of the business, not a one-off.

---

## Item 5 — Equity, dividends & buybacks: judge the capital allocation

- **The monthly share-repurchase table** — don't just note that they bought back stock; check *at what prices*. Buying back shares near highs destroys value; buying near lows creates it. This is a direct test of management's discipline.
- **Are buybacks real returns of capital, or just mopping up dilution** from stock-based comp? If share count is flat despite "billions in buybacks," they're running on a treadmill — repurchasing only to offset what they hand to employees.
- **Dividend history vs. FCF** — is the dividend comfortably covered by free cash flow, or are they borrowing/diluting to pay it?

---

## Item 7 — MD&A: the richest section, read for dissonance

This is management *narrating* the numbers. The technique is to constantly check the narration against the actual figures.

- **Decompose the revenue bridge.** How do they explain the change in revenue — volume, price, mix, FX, or acquisitions? **The quality differs enormously.** Growth from *volume* (selling more units) is healthier than growth purely from *price* (inflation pass-through that may not repeat) or from *acquisitions* (bought, not organic — and check what they paid). If they don't break this out, that itself is a tell.
- **"Critical Accounting Estimates"** — this subsection is a map of where management has the *most discretion*: revenue-recognition timing, bad-debt allowances, inventory reserves, impairment assumptions, warranty reserves. These are precisely the levers used to smooth or manage earnings. Note where the estimates are most subjective.
- **Liquidity & Capital Resources** — the debt maturity picture, available credit lines, and the standard "we believe cash is sufficient for the next 12 months." That line is boilerplate; if they *hedge* or elaborate defensively around it, worry. Watch for **covenant** mentions and a near-term **maturity wall**.
- **Non-GAAP reconciliations** — scrutinize *what they add back* to get to "adjusted" earnings. Stock-based comp added back every single year is not a one-time item — it's a real, recurring cost of paying employees. "Restructuring charges" that appear annually aren't restructuring; they're the cost of doing business. A widening GAAP-to-adjusted gap over time is a flag.
- **Tone and word choice, tracked across years.** The appearance of "challenging," "headwinds," "softness," "transitory," or the *removal* of confident language is a leading indicator. Management telegraphs deterioration in adjectives before it shows in guidance.

> **The inference move:** read MD&A with the income statement open beside it. Find the metric they emphasize and ask *what flattering thing it distracts from*. If margins fell but they only talk about revenue growth, that's the tell.

## Item 8 — Financial Statements *and the Footnotes*: where the truth lives

The statements are summaries; the footnotes are the substance. Prioritize these:

- **Revenue recognition.** *When* is revenue booked, and on what terms? Watch for bill-and-hold arrangements, percentage-of-completion (estimate-heavy), or multiple performance obligations being recognized aggressively up front. Recognition pulled forward borrows from future quarters.
- **Segment data.** This is the *real* profitability picture. Consolidated margins routinely hide a dying segment being subsidized by a great one. Look at each segment's operating margin, growth, and capex. A company "growing" on the back of one segment while another quietly bleeds is a different investment than the headline suggests.
- **Deferred revenue / contract liabilities.** For subscription and software businesses this is a *leading indicator* — growing deferred revenue means future recognized revenue is already contracted. Shrinking deferred revenue is an early warning the top line will follow.
- **Debt footnote.** The **maturity schedule** (when does the debt come due — is there a wall in the next 1–2 years they'll have to refinance at today's rates?), **fixed vs. floating** rates (floating = exposure to rate moves), and **covenants** (leverage or coverage tests that, if breached, trigger default).
- **Stock-based compensation.** The dilution trajectory — options and RSUs outstanding, the run-rate of new grants. This is a real transfer of ownership away from you, and the income statement understates its long-term dilutive cost.
- **Income taxes.** The **effective-vs-statutory rate reconciliation**. A low effective rate driven by one-time items isn't sustainable — normalize it. Note **NOL carryforwards** (shelter future profits), profit shifted to low-tax jurisdictions (reversal risk), and especially a **valuation allowance on deferred tax assets** — that's management quietly conceding it may not generate enough future profit to use those assets. A subtle pessimism signal.
- **Goodwill & intangibles.** The impairment-testing assumptions. **A company carrying goodwill close to its market capitalization is fragile** — one impairment can wipe out years of "earnings." Acquisition-heavy serial acquirers warrant extra scrutiny here.
- **Acquisitions / purchase price allocation.** How much of an acquisition price went to *goodwill* (the unallocated premium)? A lot of goodwill = they paid up for intangible hope.
- **Pension / post-retirement obligations.** Underfunded pensions are off-radar debt. The assumed discount rate and expected return on plan assets are levers that can flatter or hide the gap.
- **Related-party transactions.** Small print, big signal — self-dealing, family members on the payroll, transactions with entities the CEO controls.
- **Commitments & contingencies / off-balance-sheet items** — purchase commitments, guarantees, unconsolidated entities.
- **Subsequent events** — material things that happened after the period close but before filing.

## The Auditor's Report: don't skip it

- **The opinion type.** "Unqualified" / "clean" is normal. Anything else — qualified, adverse, or a disclaimer — is a flare.
- **Critical Audit Matters (CAMs).** The auditor is explicitly telling you which accounts were the **hardest and most judgment-laden to audit**. That maps directly onto where the risk of misstatement is highest — usually revenue recognition, goodwill, or complex reserves. Read CAMs as the auditor's own risk highlight reel.
- **Auditor identity and tenure.** A large company with a tiny no-name auditor is odd. An auditor who's been in place for decades raises independence questions; a *recent change of auditor* raises "why?" — cross-check for a disagreement disclosed on an 8-K.

## Item 9 / 9A — Controls: a material weakness is a real flag

A disclosed **material weakness in internal control over financial reporting** means the company itself admits its numbers could be materially wrong. Even a "significant deficiency" deserves attention. Note any *changes* in internal controls during the year.

## The Cross-Cutting Techniques (this is the actual skill)

These separate someone reading a 10-K from someone *analyzing* one:

1. **Read 3–5 years at once.** Trends and *narrative drift* reveal what a single year hides. Line up revenue mix, margins, share count, the risk-factor list, and the MD&A tone across years.
2. **Check the narrative against the numbers.** Every claim in Item 1 and MD&A should be corroborated by the statements. Dissonance — an upbeat narrative over deteriorating cash flows — is where the real story is.
3. **Track share count over time.** Net income can rise while *per-share* value stagnates because of relentless dilution. Always think per-share.
4. **Watch net income vs. operating cash flow over multiple years.** Earnings are an opinion; cash is a fact. A persistent and widening gap (profits up, OCF flat or down) is the single most reliable earnings-quality warning.
5. **Read the proxy (DEF 14A) alongside the 10-K** to see whether management is *paid* to do what the 10-K claims they're doing.

## Forensic Red Flags Specific to the 10-K

A condensed watchlist. None is conclusive alone, but clusters of them warrant stepping back:

- **Receivables or inventory growing materially faster than revenue** — possible channel stuffing (pushing product onto distributors to book sales) or obsolete/unsellable inventory.
- **A widening gap between net income and operating cash flow** — earnings that aren't converting to cash.
- **Rising DSO (days sales outstanding)** — customers paying slower, or revenue recognized before cash is collectible.
- **Recurring "non-recurring" or "one-time" charges** — every year. The exception that is actually the rule.
- **Aggressive cost capitalization** — capitalizing software development or other costs that should be expensed, to boost current earnings.
- **Convenient changes in accounting estimates** — extending asset useful lives, lowering reserves — that happen to lift earnings just enough to hit targets.
- **"Cookie-jar" reserves** — over-reserving in good years to quietly release in bad ones, smoothing earnings into an unnaturally steady line. *Real businesses are lumpy; suspiciously smooth earnings are engineered.*
- **Auditor change** — especially to a smaller firm, or right before/after a restatement.
- **Late filing** — an NT 10-K notification of inability to file on time.
- **Escalating structural complexity** — a proliferation of subsidiaries, special-purpose entities, or operations in tax havens with no clear business reason.
- **Management language turning promotional or defensive** — more superlatives, more "we strongly believe," more attacking of skeptics. Confident businesses report; insecure ones sell.

### How to actually run this on a real company
1. Download the **last 3–5 years** of 10-Ks (SEC EDGAR, free).
2. Read this year's **Item 1 and Risk Factors against last year's** and write down every meaningful change.
3. Read the **MD&A with the income statement open**, decomposing the revenue bridge and noting every non-GAAP add-back.
4. Work through the **footnotes** in the priority order above — revenue recognition, segments, debt maturities, stock comp, taxes, goodwill.
5. Run the **earnings-quality check**: net income vs. OCF, receivables/inventory vs. revenue, share count, all over 5 years.
6. Read the **CAMs** to see where the auditor thinks the risk is, then go verify those accounts yourself.
7. Write a paragraph answering: *does the story hold together across every section and against the cash?*
