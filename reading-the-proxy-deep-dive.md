# Reading a Proxy Statement (DEF 14A) Like an Analyst
### How to read incentives, governance, and integrity

The 10-K tells you what the business *does*. The proxy statement tells you whether the people running it are **paid and structured to act in your interest** — and it's the filing retail investors almost universally skip. That's a mistake, because governance is a *probability lever*: bad governance rarely sinks a stock by itself, but it dramatically raises the odds of the things that do — value-destroying acquisitions, relentless dilution, self-dealing, and a board that ignores you.

The entire document reduces to one question:

> **"Is management paid to do what's good for me, the shareholder — and can I do anything about it if they aren't?"**

The single most powerful technique: **compare what the 10-K says management is *doing* against what the proxy shows they're *paid* to do.** When the words and the money disagree — the 10-K touts "disciplined capital allocation" but the proxy pays for revenue growth — **believe the money.** People do what they're compensated to do.

---

## 1. Executive Compensation — the heart of the proxy

This is where most of the signal lives. Don't get lost in the dollar amounts; focus on the **structure of incentives**.

### What metrics drive the pay? (the most important question in the whole filing)
In the **Compensation Discussion & Analysis (CD&A)**, find exactly what triggers the bonuses and equity payouts. The metric *is* the strategy:

| If pay is tied to… | It incentivizes… | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| ROIC, FCF per share, relative TSR | Capital discipline, real per-share value | **Good alignment** |
| EPS growth (multi-year) | Earnings growth — decent, but watch buyback-juicing | Acceptable |
| **Revenue / total sales** | Empire-building, value-destroying acquisitions, growth at any price | **Misaligned** |
| **Adjusted EBITDA** | Ignoring capital costs; gameable add-backs | **Misaligned** |
| **"Strategic objectives" / discretionary** | Whatever the board feels like rewarding — unaccountable | **Red flag** |

### Use the Pay-versus-Performance table — the SEC handed you a gift
Since 2022 the SEC requires a table directly comparing **"compensation actually paid"** against **total shareholder return, peer TSR, net income, and a company-selected measure.** This forces the alignment (or the disconnect) into the open. **The smoking gun for a captured board is comp rising while TSR falls.** You no longer have to assemble this yourself — read it straight off the table, across the years shown.

### The structural tells inside the comp plan
- **Vesting horizon.** One-year vesting rewards short-termism; **three-to-five-year, performance-gated** equity aligns with long-term owners. Check that "performance share units" are *genuinely* performance-gated and not time-vesting dressed up as performance.
- **Goalpost-moving** — the loudest integrity alarm. Read the CD&A for targets being *lowered after a miss*, or **"discretionary" bonuses paid when the formula would have paid zero.** A board that retroactively rescues executives from their own targets works for management, not you.
- **Option repricing** — resetting underwater option strike prices when the stock falls. This bails out executives while shareholders eat the loss. Major red flag.
- **Change-of-control terms.** **Single-trigger** payouts (executives cash out the moment the company is acquired, regardless of whether they keep their jobs) incentivize *selling the company for a personal payday*. **Double-trigger** (requires both a sale *and* job loss) is the shareholder-friendly standard. Scrutinize golden-parachute sizing.
- **Perquisites and tax gross-ups.** Personal jet use, security details, club memberships — excessive perks signal an entitled culture. **Tax gross-ups** (the company paying the executive's taxes *on* their perks) are especially tone-deaf and increasingly seen as a governance failure.
- **CEO pay ratio** (CEO pay vs. median employee) — not damning on its own, but extreme ratios are a culture-and-tone signal worth noting.

## 2. Insider Ownership — skin in the game, and *who really controls the vote*

- **Actual shares owned** (in the beneficial ownership table) — not options, *owned stock*. Executives and directors with meaningful personal capital at risk are aligned with you. Token ownership means they share your upside far less than your fee/salary outflow.
- **Stock ownership guidelines** — does the company *require* executives to hold, say, 5–6× salary in stock? And are there **anti-hedging and anti-pledging** policies? If executives can hedge their holdings, they've quietly removed their own skin in the game; if they can pledge shares as loan collateral, a margin call can force a fire-sale that craters the stock.
- **Dual-class share structures — read this carefully.** Many founder-led companies issue super-voting shares (e.g., insiders' shares carry 10 votes to your 1). The founder can control the company while owning a *minority* of the economics. You are a passenger with effectively no vote. This isn't automatically disqualifying — if the founder is exceptional, you may want them in control — but understand that you are betting *entirely* on that person and have **no governance recourse** if they go astray. Always check for sunset provisions that collapse the dual class over time.

## 3. The Board of Directors — is anyone actually watching management?

The board is supposed to be *your* representative supervising management. Test whether it can do that job:

- **Independence.** What fraction of directors are genuinely independent (not employees, not affiliated)? The **audit, compensation, and nominating committees should be fully independent** — that's where the real oversight happens.
- **Combined CEO/Chairman.** When the CEO also chairs the board, they're effectively supervising themselves. Look for a **strong Lead Independent Director** as a partial counterweight; its absence concentrates power.
- **Classified / staggered board** — directors elected in staggered multi-year terms so the *entire* board can't be replaced in one election. This is an **entrenchment device**: it insulates the board from accountability and blocks takeovers. Annual election of all directors is far more shareholder-friendly.
- **Overboarding** — directors sitting on too many boards (a rough rule of thumb: more than four or five) can't meaningfully engage. 
- **Tenure and freshness** — a board where everyone has served 15+ years becomes clubby and loses independence in spirit if not in letter. You want relevant expertise and some turnover, not a trophy collection of long-tenured friends.
- **Attendance** — directors who miss meetings are disclosed; chronic absenteeism is a tell.

## 4. Related-Party Transactions — the integrity X-ray

Cross-reference this with the 10-K's related-party footnote. Look for the company transacting with insiders or entities they control: **loans to executives, leasing property from the CEO, hiring the chairman's consulting firm, employing family members at notable salaries.** Each is a value leak *and* a window into whether leadership treats the company as a shared enterprise or a personal piggy bank. A clean related-party section is a quiet green flag; a busy one warrants real skepticism.

---

## 5. The Auditor Ratification — a buried independence check

- **Auditor identity and tenure** — same logic as the 10-K; an extremely long tenure raises independence questions.
- **Audit fees vs. non-audit fees.** This is the key, under-watched number. If the auditor earns large **non-audit (consulting) fees** from the same client, its independence as a watchdog is compromised — it has a financial incentive not to antagonize management. A high non-audit-to-audit fee ratio is a real flag. Track audit fees over time, too; a sudden spike can signal new complexity or problems.

---

## 6. Shareholder Proposals, Say-on-Pay & Anti-Takeover Defenses

This section reveals how the board *treats its owners*:

- **Say-on-Pay vote results.** This advisory vote on executive compensation is a referendum on the comp plan. Support below roughly 70–80% signals a shareholder revolt. The real tell is the *response*: after a weak vote, did the board actually change the pay structure, or did it ignore the result and carry on? Ignoring a low say-on-pay vote tells you exactly whom the board answers to.
- **Shareholder proposals** — what owners are pushing for (declassify the board, separate chair/CEO, remove a poison pill) and whether the board engages constructively or dismisses them reflexively.
- **The anti-takeover / entrenchment stack** — watch for the combination of a **poison pill**, **supermajority voting requirements**, **inability of shareholders to call special meetings or act by written consent**, and a **classified board**. Any one may be defensible; *stacked together* they form a fortress that protects management from all accountability, including from a premium buyout offer you'd want accepted.
- **Proxy access** — whether shareholders can actually nominate directors. Its presence is shareholder-friendly.

## The Cross-Cutting Technique (the real skill)

1. **Read the money against the words.** Lay the proxy's incentive metrics next to the 10-K's stated strategy. Congruence is reassuring; divergence means the strategy you were told is not the one being paid for.
2. **Read three years of proxies together.** Has compensation gotten richer while performance lagged? Have targets been quietly reset? Has the board grown more entrenched? Drift over time is more revealing than any single year.
3. **Treat governance as a probability adjustment.** Strong governance doesn't make a bad business good. But weak governance meaningfully raises the odds of the specific events that destroy shareholder value — so it should widen your required margin of safety, not just sit as a footnote.

## Red Flags / Green Flags Cheat-Sheet

**Red flags**
- Comp rising while TSR falls (pay-performance disconnect)
- Incentives on revenue, adjusted EBITDA, or "discretionary" goals
- Targets lowered after a miss; discretionary bonuses replacing zeroed-out formulas
- Option repricing; single-trigger change-of-control; tax gross-ups; lavish perks
- Dual-class super-voting shares (especially with no sunset)
- Combined CEO/Chair with no lead independent director
- The entrenchment stack: classified board + poison pill + supermajority
- Related-party transactions / loans to insiders
- Low say-on-pay support that the board ignored
- High non-audit fees paid to the auditor
- Overboarded, stale, or non-independent directors
- Executives permitted to hedge or pledge their stock
- Negligible insider ownership of actual shares

**Green flags**
- Pay tied to ROIC / FCF per share / relative TSR over multi-year, performance-gated periods
- High insider ownership of real shares; strong ownership guidelines; anti-hedging/pledging rules
- Independent chair or a strong lead independent director
- Annual election of all directors (declassified board)
- Board responsive to say-on-pay results and shareholder proposals
- A clean related-party section
- A stable, reputable auditor with low non-audit fees

### How to actually run this on a real company
1. Pull the latest **DEF 14A** (SEC EDGAR — filed ahead of the annual meeting) and the prior **two years'**.
2. Go straight to the **CD&A and the Pay-versus-Performance table**: identify the incentive metrics and check pay against TSR.
3. Scan the **CD&A narrative** for goalpost-moving, discretionary payouts, repricing, and gross-ups.
4. Check the **beneficial ownership table** for real insider stakes and any **dual-class** structure.
5. Assess the **board**: independence, combined chair/CEO, classified terms, overboarding, tenure.
6. Read the **related-party** section and cross-reference the 10-K.
7. Check **audit vs. non-audit fees** and the **say-on-pay** result (and the board's response to it).
8. Inventory the **anti-takeover provisions**.
9. Write one sentence: *do the incentives and governance align with me, or protect management from me?*

> **The bottom line:** the proxy is the lie detector. A great business with a board that pays for the wrong things and shields itself from accountability will, often enough, find a way to disappoint its owners. Spend the hour — almost no one else does, and it's frequently where the decisive insight hides.
